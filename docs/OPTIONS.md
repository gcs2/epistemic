# Program and Institution Options

This document surveys the major forms SES could take. They are complementary layers, not mutually exclusive product pitches.

## Evaluation criteria

Each option should be assessed on epistemic gain, auditability, research novelty, adoption pathway, institutional legitimacy, safety, cost, and ability to learn from resolved outcomes.

## 1. Executable inquiry protocol

A local CLI and open schema represent hypotheses, evidence, models, constraints, challenges, and synthesis results.

**Strengths:** immediately buildable; reproducible; useful to researchers; creates a common interchange format.  
**Weaknesses:** relies on users to supply sensible likelihoods; limited cultural visibility.  
**Role:** foundational reference implementation.  
**Recommendation:** build first.

## 2. Agentic research harness

Multiple independently configured agents assume roles such as scout, model advocate, skeptic, translator, and auditor. They write structured artifacts into the protocol and cite their provenance.

**Strengths:** scales hypothesis generation and red-teaming; demonstrates a new research workflow.  
**Weaknesses:** simulated diversity can conceal common-model correlation; source hallucination and automation bias; substantial evaluation burden.  
**Key design rule:** agents propose and criticize; a separately auditable synthesis layer combines.

## 3. Evidence and argument knowledge graph

A graph stores claims, sources, derivations, contradictions, independence groups, causal links, and model dependencies.

**Strengths:** exposes citation laundering and hidden common ancestry; supports living reviews and incremental updates.  
**Weaknesses:** ontology design is difficult; graph complexity can exceed human comprehension.  
**Research opportunity:** calculate effective evidence diversity rather than raw citation count.

## 4. Bayesian and imprecise-probability workbench

A statistical engine supports Bayesian model averaging, hierarchical reliability models, credal sets, interval probabilities, and value-of-information analysis.

**Strengths:** rigorous uncertainty propagation; allows learned reliability.  
**Weaknesses:** likelihood elicitation and prior sensitivity; formal precision may outrun evidence.  
**Best use:** questions with repeat observations or resolvable forecasts.

## 5. Causal-model laboratory

Users construct causal DAGs, structural causal models, system-dynamics models, or agent-based simulations. Competing causal models are compared against the same evidence.

**Strengths:** makes mechanisms and interventions explicit; especially valuable for policy and counterfactual history.  
**Weaknesses:** causal identification is demanding; different model classes are difficult to compare.  
**Breakthrough possibility:** a “minimal rewrite” validator for historically coherent counterfactuals.

## 6. Forecasting and calibration network

SES inquiries that will resolve become scored forecasts. Model, contributor, and institution weights are learned by domain and task type.

**Strengths:** creates an empirical feedback loop; disciplines prestige and rhetoric.  
**Weaknesses:** many important questions resolve slowly or ambiguously; public scores invite gaming.  
**Safeguard:** never collapse performance to one universal leaderboard.

## 7. Living systematic-review platform

SES becomes infrastructure for continuously updated evidence reviews with explicit dependency graphs and minority reports.

**Strengths:** direct academic value; improves on static narrative reviews.  
**Weaknesses:** competes with established workflows and publishers; requires expert governance.  
**Promising domains:** nutrition, education, AI safety, macroeconomic policy, and conflict studies.

## 8. Public “epistemic observatory”

A civic interface presents major contested questions as robust cores, contested shells, leverage maps, and disconfirmation plans.

**Strengths:** culturally legible; could improve journalism and public reasoning.  
**Weaknesses:** highest political and reputational risk; simplification can be weaponized.  
**Requirement:** transparent institutional pluralism and appeal mechanisms.

## 9. Deliberation and citizen-assembly instrument

Participants contribute evidence, lived experience, values, and challenges. The tool keeps empirical synthesis distinct from normative choice.

**Strengths:** joins expert and situated knowledge; democratic legitimacy.  
**Weaknesses:** moderation, representation, and power asymmetries.  
**Novelty:** minority reports become first-class outputs rather than procedural failures.

## 10. Research benchmark and epistemic wind tunnel

Create blinded cases with known outcomes, planted dependencies, deceptive sources, missing hypotheses, and cross-disciplinary translation problems.

**Strengths:** permits comparative evaluation of humans, institutions, and AI systems.  
**Weaknesses:** benchmark leakage; historical “ground truth” is sometimes contested.  
**Recommendation:** build early, before adding sophisticated agents.

## 11. Interoperability standard

Define an open SES exchange format for claims, evidence, provenance, likelihoods, forecasts, and revisions. Other tools can produce or consume SES packets.

**Strengths:** impact larger than one application; reduces institutional lock-in.  
**Weaknesses:** standards work is slow and politically negotiated.  
**Potential lineage:** JSON-LD/PROV-O integration, signed provenance, content-addressed evidence.

## 12. Personal epistemic notebook

A private tool helps individuals reason about medical choices, careers, purchases, relationships, and beliefs.

**Strengths:** immediate utility and adoption.  
**Weaknesses:** high-stakes personal advice; subjective probabilities; privacy.  
**Design principle:** reveal value judgments and defer appropriately to qualified professionals.

## 13. Institutional audit service

Organizations apply SES to strategic decisions, postmortems, intelligence estimates, or grant portfolios.

**Strengths:** funding pathway; measurable decision outcomes.  
**Weaknesses:** proprietary capture and secrecy could conflict with public epistemic aims.

## 14. Cultural form

Interactive essays, museum installations, classrooms, games, and documentary experiences let people explore how conclusions change with evidence and assumptions.

**Strengths:** builds epistemic intuition beyond specialist communities.  
**Weaknesses:** narrative engagement can reward drama over calibration.  
**Cultural opportunity:** make changing one’s mind visible and honorable.

## 15. Federated epistemic commons

Independent universities, newsrooms, communities, laboratories, and civil-society groups publish signed SES analyses. A federation compares convergence and diagnoses disagreement without creating a central truth authority.

**Strengths:** resilience, legitimacy, pluralism, resistance to capture.  
**Weaknesses:** governance and identity are difficult; adversarial manipulation.  
**Long-term recommendation:** the institution-scale destination.

## Recommended sequence

### Phase 0 — Protocol and benchmark

- Publish the founding document and inquiry schema.
- Build the deterministic reference harness.
- Create blinded benchmark cases and scoring rules.
- Establish documentation for uncertainty and minority reports.

### Phase 1 — Research workbench

- Add provenance graphs, source snapshots, and causal diagrams.
- Add notebooks and a visual interface.
- Support Bayesian, imprecise-probability, and qualitative synthesis modes.
- Run retrospective studies with interdisciplinary teams.

### Phase 2 — Agentic augmentation

- Add independently configured research roles.
- Measure genuine diversity, source overlap, and correlated error.
- Require citations, reproducible traces, and an auditor agent from a separate model family where possible.
- Keep all automatically suggested weights distinguishable from human-approved weights.

### Phase 3 — Prospective calibration

- Register forecasts before resolution.
- Learn performance by domain, horizon, and claim type.
- Publish failures and model revisions.
- Compare SES teams with conventional expert panels and prediction markets.

### Phase 4 — Public federation

- Establish multi-institution governance.
- Publish interoperable signed inquiry packets.
- Develop appeal, privacy, and high-risk inquiry procedures.
- Create public observatory and cultural interfaces.

## Highest-leverage research questions

1. How can evidence dependence be estimated when citation and data ancestry are incomplete?
2. When does multi-agent debate produce independent error correction rather than theatrical disagreement?
3. How should qualitative testimony enter probabilistic synthesis without erasing context?
4. Can weights be learned without entrenching historically measurable but socially narrow outcomes?
5. How should unresolved deep uncertainty be represented: probabilities, intervals, scenarios, or explicit ignorance?
6. What interaction design best communicates a robust core and contested shell?
7. Can causal coherence be automatically checked without imposing one discipline’s ontology?
8. What governance prevents an epistemic platform from becoming a political legitimacy machine?

## The academically significant contribution

The strongest scholarly contribution would not be another ensemble algorithm. It would be a tested theory of **dependence-aware, cross-disciplinary, adversarial synthesis** with an open protocol and comparative benchmarks.

## The culturally significant contribution

The strongest cultural contribution would be a shared practice in which people can see not only a conclusion, but its provenance, uncertainty, strongest objection, minority report, and path to revision—and in which updating is treated as intellectual integrity rather than defeat.
